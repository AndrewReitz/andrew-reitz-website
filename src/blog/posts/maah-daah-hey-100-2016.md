The [Maah Daah Hey 100](https://t.umblr.com/redirect?z=http%3A%2F%2Fwww.experienceland.org%2Fmaah-daah-hey-100%2F&amp;t=ZWY0NjJkMDQyODQ5YmFjMDg1Y2NjNGNlNDEyYjZiMTRjYjIzNDMzMyxUOFluS1BOVA%3D%3D&amp;b=t%3Afk0PV5o1P6AGonrFjBJeJg&amp;p=http%3A%2F%2Fadventure.andrewreitz.com%2Fpost%2F150086973784%2Fmaah-daah-hey-100-2016&amp;m=1) is a 107-mile race through the badlands of North Dakota on the Maah Daah Hey trail. The race is held the first weekend of August and held at this time so that the Little Missouri River that racers must cross, is at it’s lowest. Oh did I mention there is a river crossing? The heat gets to be around 100 ℉, and in 2015 only about 30% of the people that entered the race finished. Sounds like my kind of adventure.




![image](https://cdn-images-1.medium.com/max/800/0*5CVfRRglp8v7jlRK.jpg)



_The badlands_

I first heard about the race from a friend I mountain bike with. She described it to me and it sounded crazy. I immediately looked it up when I got home. It quickly became something I really wanted to do and was added to my list of goals for 2016. The race required that someone be able to pick you up at the end, or whenever you end up call it a day, it’s in one direction and not a loop, which made the likelihood of me being able to accomplish this goal a bit harder. I asked my mom to be my support, but she was supposed to be busy helping my sister move, and even though I was the only one of the family to show up on mother’s day and take her out to brunch (yes mother I’m never going to let them live this down) she wouldn’t go with me out to North Dakota. I started to ask other people that I thought might be in the race if they would drive me back to my car, but no luck. I was starting to get desperate and was even thinking about posting on the Maah Daah Hey 100 Facebook page to see if anyone would be willing to give me a ride when I decided to ask my girlfriend, Ashley if she would go with me.

Why didn’t I ask her before? Well, we recently started dating, and I wasn’t sure how well asking her to support me on this adventure would go. After bringing it up and saying I would pay for the hotels and whatnot she agreed to be my support. Ashley, thank you so much for making sure I didn’t die!




![image](https://cdn-images-1.medium.com/max/800/0*-LTYGcLMU2sJAEWO.jpg)



_Parker of the Angry Catfish setting off on the trail_

I like crazy endurances races but the real reason I wanted to do this race, and the reason I like doing other endurance races is because I think they are fun. I don’t think I will ever be super fast, at least not fast enough to place in a race, and don’t particularly find road riding interesting, but on gravel or trails you get beautiful views and a great challenge. Just finishing these type of races in once piece is reward enough for me.

I also really like getting out of my boring day to day routines. Waking up with the feeling my stomach is in knots and heart is pounding is for lack of a better word is enjoyable to me. This spawns from a new year’s resolution I came up with a few years back based off of a quote a pro snowboarder said. My resolution was “to scare the shit out of myself daily”. Simply put, to push myself out of my comfort zone and do cool new things. To be more like that movie Yes Man. Although I realize daily isn’t practical, doing my best to really get out of my comfort zone is fun and a continual goal.

For training, I biked a lot. Mostly mountain biked, but also spent some time on the trainer if the weather was bad or I didn’t have enough time to get out to the trails. The trainer workouts were more targeted at increasing my VO2 max and to be ready for all the hills. There is A LOT of climbing, like 12,000 feet of elevation gain. For mountain bike riding/training, the goal was to try and go fast and to get faster every time. Work was pretty crazy around this time and was seriously cutting into my training which was unfortunate but it also helped pay for this endeavor, so with the good comes the bad. In order to balance training, overtime at work, a girlfriend, a dog and a 6-year-old, I had to try and be efficient and somewhat smart about what I would train. This, unfortunately, meant that the amount of time I spent at the climbing gym decreased significantly. Where I was going anywhere from 2 to 4 times a week on a normal week I was now down to 0 to 1 times a week. I was still trying to keep up with my core work and shoulder work which would still be useful on the bike but I would only try to maintain not build upper body muscles.

I was sort of freaking out after signing up for this race. Realizing the finishing rate was so low and freaked about water and food I managed to come into contact with Parker at the Angry Catfish bike shop. He did this race in 2015 and gave me lots of good information that put my mind at ease. Some of that important stuff for any of you reading this to learn more about what to expect is as follows. Water is at every aid station and checkpoint, and some of the aid stations have Cokes and HEED sports drinks. Some checkpoints have food, and all aid stations have food. The food was bananas, pickles, and chips. I didn’t spend too much time checking out the food so there might be other options. There is a lot of climbing, pace yourself. Take a large rest at aid station 2, because the 3rd section of the trail is the toughest. This is not because the trail is actually any more difficult, but because the time of day it will be hot and miserable, take more water than you think you need through here. Thanks Parker for sharing all that information with me!




![image](https://cdn-images-1.medium.com/max/800/0*ygsB7xShw89Aw9zm.jpg)



_Packing My Gear_

The weekend of the race was finally here. It was something I was both dreading and excited for. Ashley came over Thursday night and we loaded her car up so that we could take off early Friday morning. That night I couldn’t sleep. I ended up waking up sometime before 5 am and just laying there awake. Finally forcing myself to get out of bed around 6am, this allowed me to get an early start on breakfast and packing up the final items. We took the dog for a walk (he would be staying behind with my brother) and got on the road around 8am. We would be heading to the Roosevelt Inn and Suites in Watford City North Dakota. Nothing of interest happened on our drive, I mostly read and slept, and we arrived at our destination some 9 hours later.

We checked into the hotel and I checked in for the race. We run into a few people we know, and I get some last minute tips for the next day as well as get Ashley ready for the support driving. Turns out she also would be crossing the river. Good thing we took her Rav 4 and not my Ford Focus. We head out for some food at a little sushi restaurant, where I eat lots of veggie sushi. I was very cautious about what I wanted to eat and wanted to make sure it wasn’t too heavy. We head back to the hotel and watch the opening ceremony for the Olympics where I fell asleep around 9pm. The race starts at 7am so I would get up at 5am to make sure I’ve eaten, out to the start, and ready to roll by that time.




![image](https://cdn-images-1.medium.com/max/800/0*amXfj6zNfgv0zJ8Z.jpg)



_Pictures of previous races in our hotel room_

My goal for this race was to finish before dark, which didn’t seem too unreasonable, but also going into what I have heard as the hardest mountain bike race, I wasn’t quite sure what to expect. All I knew was I didn’t want to be out there with headlamps trying to finish the trail. This left me with about 14 hours of daylight.

I wake up about every hour on the hour that night. Waking up and jumping out of bed right before the alarm goes off at 5am. I start packing everything back up from the hotel room, putting on my kit, and drinking my Skratch Labs hyperhydration mix. I eat a bagel while topping off our coolers with ice. We roll into CCC campground as many others pull in. I start getting everything ready on my bike, checking the tire pressures, putting on my Revelate Design Gas Can and Jerry Can. I meet some of the other riders and then it’s time to line up. As we head to the starting line I hear another rider talking to himself saying “this is crazy, this is crazy.”




![image](https://cdn-images-1.medium.com/max/800/0*CEYuec3BxCcvF8cw.jpg)



_Getting read before the race_

We line up with the fastest in the front and slower in the back. Nick, the race director gives some last minute information about the race and then a [Theodore Roosevelt](https://t.umblr.com/redirect?z=https%3A%2F%2Fwww.facebook.com%2FMaahDaahHey100%2Fvideos%2F1068580013228847%2F&amp;t=NjZiNTVjOWJmZTc0ZTQ2YzIwMmVkYTYwNTNiNWI1Mzg2MTExMDk1OSxUOFluS1BOVA%3D%3D&amp;b=t%3Afk0PV5o1P6AGonrFjBJeJg&amp;p=http%3A%2F%2Fadventure.andrewreitz.com%2Fpost%2F150086973784%2Fmaah-daah-hey-100-2016&amp;m=1) impersonator gives us a very motivational speech. With a few minutes to spare Nick says a prayer (he does this in a way that is very respectful of others beliefs) asking to watch over everyone involved in the race. Then we are off! Down the short gravel road of the campground the north most point of the Maah Daah Hey trail. We are all single file as we hit the beginning of the trail. We are entering what is known as the never ending switchbacks. I heard someone talking about these before the race and I was terrified, it turns out they weren’t that bad. Just go slow and try not to run into the other people in front of you. One thing I learned during this first part of the race, is if everyone is walking their bike uphill, you probably should too.

We all stay pretty bunched up until we get to the top of the switchbacks. For a while, after the switchbacks, it is just a large grassland with lots of cows. This is where the group starts to split up and I stay on the back of some guys that are all riding together. It’s good to have some people in front of me to force me to pace myself. There are lots of cattle gates as we continue on. These really slow you down and are annoying. You need to jump off your bike, lift the gate, push your bike through while keeping the gate up, then let it slam behind you as you try to get back all that momentum you lost.




![image](https://cdn-images-1.medium.com/max/800/0*fPdd_tZyubNYlMC5.jpg)



_Having fun on the trail_

The trail is beautiful and just out of this world in spots. Other areas are just a grassland and aren’t really much to talk about. The actual trail itself is bumpy and really requires a lot of focus and attention to ride. I quickly learned that if you can not see around a corner of a down hill you need to slow way down. I almost flew off a cliff or two in the first section of trail. I wish I could describe the scenery of the trial to you, but I can’t, the best thing I can do is post a few pictures of how bizarre it really is. You really should check it out for yourself. There is white, almost ash like dirt, there are sections of bright red dirt, parts that are very dessert like and even a few cactus next to the trail. There are sections that have cows and horses right next to you with cattle prints across the trail making for an extremely bumping ride. There are little, very muddy creeks you have to roll through, some forest sections, and some very awesome downhill sections around the crazy landscape there is.

The first checkpoint is about 12 miles in. At this point, I don’t really need much of anything. I made it, I still have water, I continue on. The next stop is the first aid station, roughly ¼th of the way down the trail. Here I try to eat, use the restroom, and tune up my bike before getting back onto the trail. Eating is tough as it’s getting hotter, and for some reason, I don’t really want solid food. My plan the whole race was to eat roughly 300 kcal an hour. This has quickly gone out the window since I have no idea what time it is and really have only been eating GU whenever I can remember. I don’t take too long of a break and am quickly back on my back after cleaning off the chain and derailleur a bit.




![image](https://cdn-images-1.medium.com/max/800/0*2OS9T_AF1aDCYlwB.jpg)



Between aid station 1 and aid station 2 there are two checkpoints. These checkpoints have water, a way for you to get access to your support vehicles. Aid stations have your drop bags, food, water, restrooms, and a way to get to your support vehicles. I’m not sure which checkpoint but at one of them I was not feeling too great at this point and take a rest. I drink some water and try to eat more food. It’s difficult to eat. Eventually, at the top of a large hill, I stop and take a break. The view is great, I feel like crap, and something finally switched where I am hungry. I devour all the food I have on me. Two laura bars, some GU energy chews, and some more GU gel, Nothing like eating a thousand or so calories in one sitting. In between these checkpoints I’m starting to finish off my 2-liter hydration pack. That’s roughly a little less than 2 liters per hour!

Before making the journey down to the river you go across the very top points of some hills. Move too far to the left, and you’re taking a tumble, same goes for the right. The breeze is great, and I almost feel cold. This is probably my favorite section of the trail. It was gorgeous. Just before the second aid station is the river crossing, and I know I’m getting close as I continue to descend down hills into the valley. I roll up to the crossing, sit down, pull off my shoes, throw my bike over my right shoulder, grab my shoes in my left hand, and just walk across like I’ve done this before. After being so hot and sweaty for the last 6 hours (that’s an estimate) on the bike, the water feels great, along with the mud squishing between your toes. I feel back to 100 percent, and ready to tackle the rest of the trail.

On the other side of the river is the only person around, another man doing the race putting his shoes on. We get to talking, and when I learn that he has been biking every day for the past 7 days at different trails, I said surprisingly “whoa, you biked the day before this?” He responds, “yeah, it’s just biking.” I don’t remember what this man looks like, or what else we talked about, but this really stood out to me.

I continue on from the river up the hill to the second aid station. I drink water, fill up my hydration pack, eat some feed, although probably not as much as I should have, and just sit and rest. As I sit there in the shade of the easy up, I hear one of the aid station volunteers say something about how the station is only open for another two hours. For the first time since the race has started I actually care what time it is. I quickly ask Ashley what time it is and she says it’s 1pm. I have been on the bike for 7 hours. I have 7 more hours to go to make my goal / before dark. This freaks me out a bit and I hurriedly set out after hearing this.

I do take an extra water bottle, so now I have about 2.5 liters of water on me. There are 3 checkpoints between aid station 2 and 3. The longest gap being 9 miles. The first checkpoint is 7 miles away, and I say out loud, “7 miles, I can do that!” And I can, but there are a few major mistakes I made here that will end up being my downfall.

First, I forgot to tune up my bike. Clean it off and put more lube on the chain. Not the worst mistake but it does make pedaling a lot harder when your bike it caked in mud. The second, and everyone told me to take my time at aid station 2, was drink lots of water, and really make sure to refuel as the 3rd leg is the hardest leg. I rested, ate, and drank, but not nearly as much as I should have. Finally, I have been drinking 2 liters of water between each checkpoint, again very roughly 2 liters per hour, and yet I have not had to pee since the first aid station. This should have been a red flag that I should have drank more, especially some electrolyte drinks. I should have drank until I peed. The reason I was avoiding sports drinks is I can’t stand how sweet they are when I’m really thirsty, and I thought I had had enough electrolytes from the GU and ultra hydration mix.

From the second aid station to the next checkpoint, I quickly realize my bike is having issues. I’m having to walk many of the hills and the sound of the drivetrain is driving me crazy, but I push through to the next checkpoint. This is where I begin to feel “weird” for lack of a better description. I don’t want to eat any more energy gel or anything sweet, so I ask Ashley for a piece of bread. I eat a single slice of bread and drink water while working on my bike, Nothing too out of the ordinary. I still feel ok, although a bit loopy, but continue on without too much of a break.

The next checkpoint is 9 miles away. 9 miles shouldn’t be difficult, I can bike 9 miles most days without even thinking about it, Somewhere from the last checkpoint to the next, everything goes south. I feel bad, really bad. My legs are wobbly, and I’m having to walk up every hill. A few points I stop and just stand to rest on my bike. I drink all of my water in my hydration pack and am only left with 0.5 liters in my water bottle. I keep getting a sweat sunscreen mixture in my eyes and they are burning, but I don’t want to waste any water on that. I keep pressing forwards, slow, and feeling like I’m going to throw up.

I come to a wooded section, and see a man just sitting there. I ask how he’s doing (he didn’t look great), and he said he just needs to get his heart rate down. I ask if I can do anything, and he asks for some water. I let him have a drink from what little I have left and I continue forwards. I wish there was more I could do for him, but I’m not too far away from being in the same shape he’s in. I push on up some hills. Going downhill is faster, but painful. My stomach is bouncing all around in pain. I keep thinking I’ll see the checkpoint soon, but it seems to take forever.

I finally make it. Ashley is right by the exit of the trail, I drop my bike and try to throw up by the side of her car. I ask her to turn the car on and blast cool air at me. I hop in. I keep jumping out to throw up. It never happens. I keep telling myself when I feel better I will continue on, but I think already by this time mentally I’m too far gone. I drink more water and cool off, but I think I just didn’t pace myself. I slowly start stripping clothing. Pulling my socks and shoes off feel great. My socks are so caked in mud that they actually molded to my feet, and it took Ashley and I pulling them to get them off.




![image](https://cdn-images-1.medium.com/max/800/0*BQsAQq56e7KDYtts.jpg)



_Trying to cool down in the car. After race selfie?_

I throw in the towel, and we work our way to our campsite for the night in Medora ND. The best way to describe how I feel is “weird”. I do not feel normal. I feel as though I haven’t slept in days, almost similar to coming down from a trip. There is no other way to describe it. Pretty sure It really came down to dehydration and not pacing myself. I had a great time, and will be there next year and will finish. I really hope everyone made it out safely, I want to thank the volunteers and staff that help put the Maah Daah Hey together, and I really enjoyed meeting all the new people out on the trail. Happy trails and until next year.
